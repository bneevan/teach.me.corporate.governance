CG-001 · Lesson 01 · Conflicts: Managed, Not Erased

A director's relative offers the best market price and superior quality for a critical contract. Does that commercial merit eliminate the conflict of interest? Must the company automatically reject the transaction?

Reveal answer

No to both.

The personal relationship—and therefore the structural conflict of interest—remains intact regardless of commercial price or quality advantages. The transaction does not have to be rejected, but it cannot proceed through standard managerial discretion.

It can lawfully proceed only if the conflict is formally disclosed, evaluated by disinterested directors, stripped of the interested director's participation or vote, and approved by the Audit Committee, Board, or shareholders under applicable statutory procedures.

A company may accept a transaction involving a conflict without accepting conflicted decision-making.

Key distinction: Commercial merits and process integrity are distinct inquiries. A great deal never legalizes procedural non-compliance; a pristine procedure cannot legalize a prohibited transaction.

Primary Source: Companies Act 2013 §§ 177 (Audit Committee), 184 (Disclosure of Interest), 188 (Related Party Transactions).

CG-002 · Lesson 02 · Board Voting vs. Shareholding Power

A founder owns 60% of the voting equity shares and sits on a 5-member board of directors. Does the founder hold 60% of the voting power during a board resolution?

Reveal answer

No.

Shareholding percentages govern shareholder general meetings (AGMs and EGMs). At the board table, voting is strictly per-capita (one director, one vote). On a 5-member board, each director commands exactly 20% of the voting power (1 of 5 votes).

Unless valid articles provide specific reserved matters or statutory casting votes to the Chair, board decisions pass by the governing procedural rule (e.g., simple majority of eligible, disinterested directors present and voting), not weighted share percentages or forced consensus.

The person is the same; the powers, duties, and voting mechanics of each chair are not.

Primary Source: Companies Act 2013 § 173; ICSI Secretarial Standard on Meetings of the Board of Directors (SS-1).

CG-003 · Lesson 03 · Duty of Care vs. Duty of Loyalty

What is the core distinction between the Duty of Care and the Duty of Loyalty? If an honest director with pure intentions approves a complex acquisition without reading the financial disclosures, which duty is breached?

Reveal answer

Breach of the Duty of Care.

  • Duty of Care: An objective standard requiring directors to exercise reasonable diligence, skill, independent inquiry, and care that a prudent person would exercise under similar circumstances (§ 166(2)). It tests the thoroughness of the decision process.
  • Duty of Loyalty: An unyielding obligation to act in good faith exclusively in the best interests of the company, prohibiting self-dealing, secret profits, exploiting corporate opportunities, or placing personal interests above the enterprise (§ 166(3), (5)).

A director can be entirely loyal (harboring zero personal gain or malice) while still committing a severe breach of the Duty of Care by rubber-stamping matters without diligent review or inquiry.

Loyalty tests allegiances and unconflicted purpose; Care tests diligence, competence, and decision process.

Primary Source: Companies Act 2013 § 166(2) (Care & Diligence), § 166(3) (Conflict of Interest & Undue Gain).

CG-004 · Lesson 03 · Audit Committee Composition & Oversight

Why does corporate law mandate an Audit Committee with a majority of Independent Directors and financial literacy? Why can't the executive board members handle financial audit oversight directly?

Reveal answer

To prevent self-auditing and manage inherent management conflicts.

Executive management and executive directors prepare financial accounts, manage expenditures, and control daily operations. Permitting them to oversee their own audit creates an unmanageable structural conflict of interest.

An independent Audit Committee (§ 177) acts as an autonomous structural barrier: it interfaces directly with external statutory auditors, oversees internal financial controls, reviews accounting policies, and independently evaluates Related Party Transactions (RPTs) before they can be considered by the full board or shareholders.

Those who prepare and spend the numbers cannot be the sole arbiters of their integrity.

Primary Source: Companies Act 2013 § 177; SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations 2015 Reg. 18.

CG-005 · Lesson 04 · Principal-Agent (Type I) vs. Principal-Principal (Type II) Agency Conflicts

How does the primary corporate governance agency conflict in the US/UK (dispersed ownership) differ from that in India and Continental Europe (concentrated ownership)?

Reveal answer

They face fundamentally different agency problems based on ownership structure:

  • Type I (Principal-Agent): Characteristic of the US/UK Berle-Means model of dispersed shareholding. Thousands of small, dispersed shareholders (principals) face collective action problems monitoring entrenched professional managers (agents) who may prioritize empire-building, executive compensation, or risk-aversion.
  • Type II (Principal-Principal):** Characteristic of concentrated/promoter ownership in India, Continental Europe, and East Asia. Controlling promoters or majority shareholders (dominant principals) control board appointments and management, creating the risk that they extract private benefits of control at the expense of minority shareholders (vulnerable principals) via tunneling or unscrutinized related-party transactions.
US/UK governance guards against managers looting passive owners; Indian/European governance guards against controlling promoters squeezing minority investors.

Analytical Framework: Berle & Means (1932) vs. Concentrated Ownership Paradigm (La Porta et al., 1999).

CG-006 · Lesson 04 · Unitary vs. Two-Tier Boards & Co-Determination (Mitbestimmung)

How does the German two-tier board system structurally divide corporate power, and how does Co-Determination (Mitbestimmung) alter board composition?

Reveal answer

Through institutional separation of management from supervision, and mandatory employee representation:

  • Two-Tier Separation: Corporate leadership is split into two distinct bodies. The Vorstand (Management Board) exclusively handles operational leadership. The Aufsichtsrat (Supervisory Board) appoints, monitors, and advises the Management Board. Simultaneous membership on both boards is strictly prohibited by law.
  • Co-Determination (Mitbestimmung): In German corporations with more than 2,000 employees, 50% of the Supervisory Board members are elected by employees and trade unions (with the shareholder-elected Chair retaining the casting tie-breaking vote). This legally hard-codes labor and enterprise sustainability into high-level corporate governance alongside financial capital.
Anglo-American governance unites managers and supervisors in one room; German governance places them in separate chambers and gives workers half the supervisory seats.

Comparative Framework: German Stock Corporation Act (Aktiengesetz / AktG) & Co-Determination Act 1976 (Mitbestimmungsgesetz).

CG-007 · Lesson 05 · Retained Accountability in Autonomous AI Delegation

If a corporate board authorizes an autonomous AI agent for financial transactions or procurement and the agent incurs disastrous losses without guardrails, can directors escape liability by claiming "the algorithm acted autonomously"?

Reveal answer

No.

Corporate fiduciary duties establish that while a board can lawfully delegate operational tasks to executives or AI agents, retained fiduciary accountability cannot be delegated away.

Under the Duty of Care (§ 166(2)), directors and officers maintain an affirmative, non-delegable oversight obligation: they must establish clear boundary conditions, verify pre-deployment testing, conduct ongoing supervisory monitoring, and maintain functional human-in-the-loop override mechanisms ("circuit breakers") before deploying autonomous agents.

You can delegate operational execution; you can never delegate away retained fiduciary accountability.

Governance Precedents: In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation (systemic oversight); Companies Act 2013 § 166(2).
