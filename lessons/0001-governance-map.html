Lesson 01 · Primer + baseline assessment · About 12 minutes

Who may decide—and who must answer?

Our goal: understand corporate governance before extending it to AI agents. India is our reference point, not an exception to a supposedly universal Western model.

Today's tangible win: distinguish ownership, governance, and management in a simple company scenario. The country map is orientation, not material to memorize.

1. More than guardrails

Corporate governance is the system through which a company is directed, controlled, and held accountable. It establishes who has authority, how decisions are supervised, whose interests must be considered, and how people must answer for their conduct. It includes setting direction—not merely preventing misconduct. Compare the German Code's definition and the UK Code's five areas.

A company is legally distinct from its shareholders. Buying its shares does not make its bank account your personal bank account. Governance allocates authority within that separate organization; it does not, by itself, create its legal personality. In India, start with Companies Act section 9.

FunctionIllustrative question
GovernanceWho may authorize a major investment, within what limits, with what oversight?
ManagementHow do we deliver the approved investment?
ComplianceWhat legal obligations and approvals must we satisfy?
OperationsWho places the orders and runs the process?

These activities overlap. The distinction concerns functions, not necessarily four separate departments.

2. India's basic authority map

Shareholders / members
  vote on director appointments and specified reserved matters
          ↕ accountability, voting, reporting
Board of directors — collective governing authority
  ↔ committees supporting particular oversight responsibilities
          ↕ delegation, supervision, reporting
CEO / managing director and executive management
          ↕ delegated operating authority
Teams and day-to-day operations

This is an introductory map, not an exhaustive legal organization chart. In India, see sections 152, 166, 177–180, and 203. Powers depend on company type, articles, law, and applicable rules; some matters require shareholder approval.

  • Shareholders: hold ownership rights and vote on specified matters; they do not automatically direct every employee.
  • Board: exercises collective powers, directs and oversees, and supervises delegated management. Directors must exercise judgment, care, and attention to conflicts.
  • Chair: leads the board's work. The title does not confer unlimited personal authority.
  • Executives: run the business within the authority granted to them. A CEO or managing director may also sit on the board.
  • Committees: support focused work such as audit oversight or nomination and remuneration where applicable. They do not erase the board's responsibilities.

A founder can simultaneously be a shareholder, director, and CEO. The person is the same; the powers and duties of each role are not. Auditors, regulators, and courts provide additional checks; they are not merely successive rungs above the CEO.

3. What responsibilities does governance cover?

Think in six buckets: direction, leadership, risk, reporting, conflicts, and accountability. These are a teaching synthesis, not six statutory departments. They include overseeing strategy, executive appointment and incentives, controls, reliable disclosure, and decisions affected by competing interests. The UK Code provides a useful organizing framework.

India's section 166 makes an important correction to a common misconception: directors' duties are not simply “obey the largest shareholder.” It refers to promoting company objects for members as a whole and the interests of the company, employees, shareholders, community, and environmental protection, alongside independent judgment and conflict restrictions. That does not give every stakeholder equal voting rights.

Scenario: An Indian company's founder wants it to purchase services from a family-connected business. The governance questions are not just “Is the price attractive?” but “Is there a conflict? Who must disclose it? Who can review and approve? What evidence and reporting are required?” Exact approval obligations depend on the facts and applicable rules; see sections 184 and 188.

4. Compare systems against India

For an introductory comparison, focus on larger companies and public-company governance, while recognizing that legal forms, listing status, ownership, and sectors change the rules. This table identifies structural contrasts—not rankings or claims about every business.
