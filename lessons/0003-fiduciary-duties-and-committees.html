Fiduciary Duties & Board Committees

Your tangible win: distinguish the Duty of Care from the Duty of Loyalty, and explain why an independent Audit Committee exists even when the full board has final authority.

First, retrieve—not reread

From memory: when a 5-member board votes on a matter within its powers, why can't a founder owning 60% of the company's shares simply outvote the other four directors single-handedly?

Review the previous principle

A shareholding vote takes place in a general meeting of shareholders; at the board table, each director holds one vote. Share ownership percentage does not multiply individual board votes.

1. The Twin Pillars: Care vs. Loyalty

Directors stand in a fiduciary relationship with the company. A fiduciary is someone entrusted to hold power and make decisions on behalf of another. In corporate law, this obligation splits into two fundamental duties:

Fiduciary Duty What it demands Core failure mode
Duty of Care Act with due diligence, skill, inquiry, and reasonable prudence before making decisions. Read the briefings, ask hard questions, understand risks. Gross negligence, laziness, rubber-stamping proposals without reading or asking questions.
Duty of Loyalty Put the company’s best interests first. Act in good faith for the benefit of members as a whole. Do not appropriate corporate opportunities or enrich yourself or relatives at company expense. Conflict of interest, self-dealing, insider trading, secretly diverting business to friends/relatives.

In India, section 166 of the Companies Act, 2013 codifies these duties explicitly:

  • Section 166(2): Act in good faith to promote the company's objects for members as a whole, considering employees, shareholders, community, and the environment. (Loyalty & broad purpose)
  • Section 166(3): Exercise duties with due and reasonable care, skill, diligence, and independent judgment. (Care)
  • Section 166(4) & (5): Avoid direct or indirect conflicts of interest, and do not achieve or attempt to achieve undue gain or advantage. (Loyalty)

2. Who sits in the boardroom?

Not all directors have the same day-to-day role in company operations:

Director Type Role Key function
Executive Director (ED) Full-time employee (e.g., Managing Director, CEO, CFO). Runs day-to-day business operations; designs and executes strategy.
Non-Executive Director (NED) Not an employee; often represents major investors or founders. Provides strategic guidance and perspective; does not manage daily operations.
Independent Director (ID) A non-executive with no material or pecuniary relationship with the company, promoter, or management. Brings objective, dispassionate scrutiny; protects public and minority shareholder interests against promoter self-dealing.

3. Why Committees? Deep scrutiny and conflict insulation

A full board meeting with 12 directors cannot audit thousands of ledger entries or evaluate executive bonus formulas in detail. Boards delegate focused scrutiny to specialized committees:

The Audit Committee (Companies Act Section 177)

  • Must have at least 3 directors, with independent directors forming the majority.
  • The chairperson must have financial literacy.
  • Key responsibilities:
    1. Review integrity of financial statements and disclosures.
    2. Oversee statutory auditor selection, compensation, and independence.
    3. Evaluate internal financial controls and risk management systems.
    4. Review and approve Related Party Transactions (RPTs) before they go forward.
Why a majority of Independent Directors? If executive management or the majority shareholder (promoter) wants to enter into a contract with their own private firm, independent directors ensure the transaction is priced at fair market value (arm's length) and genuinely serves the company.

4. Connecting to AI Agents

When autonomous agents are introduced into company operations:

  • Duty of Care: If directors approve autonomous AI loan-approval agents without inquiring into bias, error rates, model validation, and rollback mechanisms, they risk breaching their Duty of Care. "The algorithm decided" is not a defense for failing to exercise reasonable inquiry.
  • Duty of Loyalty: If an executive secretly deploys an agent that routes company procurement orders exclusively to vendor accounts they control, it is a flagrant breach of the Duty of Loyalty.
  • Audit & Risk Oversight: Agent workflows require algorithmic audit trails, logging, and threshold limits that report into internal controls reviewed by the Audit/Risk Committee.
